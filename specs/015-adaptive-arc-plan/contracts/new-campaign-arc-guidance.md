```markdown
## 4a. Designing the arc

A homebrew arc is built on a story structure, named on the Arc plan's first line. The full save reads this step when it plans the next arc or revises one.

**The Five Commandments** (Shawn Coyne, *The Story Grid*), the default. One stage per beat, except progressive complication, which may take one to three stages, each costing more than the last:

- **Inciting incident**: an event, someone's choice or pure chance, that upsets the balance of the character's world for good or ill, and that they cannot ignore.
- **Progressive complication**: each step to restore the balance makes things harder. It ends in a turning point, an action or a revelation, that forces the crisis.
- **Crisis**: a real choice between options that cannot both be had: the best of bad choices, or two goods. Plan the dilemma, never the answer: the player chooses.
- **Climax**: the player's answer to the crisis, played out; usually the arc's largest scene.
- **Resolution**: what follows from that choice, for the world, its people and the character.

**The Hero's Journey** (Joseph Campbell; Christopher Vogler's twelve stages), for an arc that is the character's own change: the ordinary world, the call, the refusal, the mentor, the threshold; tests, allies and enemies, the approach, the ordeal, the reward; the road back, the resurrection, the return with the elixir. Group them into five to seven stages, each naming the journey stages it covers. Its stages also suit a character arc inside a Five Commandments arc.

**Plan situations, not plots.** Each stage says what changes and who wants what, never how the party gets there. Put each character arc's test on a turning point or the crisis, so the main plot forces it. The five beats also shape a good scene (something upsets it, things get harder, a choice, its answer, what follows): use them to make scenes turn, not to script them.

**Budgets.** Give each stage a rough number of sessions, from how long this player's stages have run (About this player); a crisis or a resolution is usually one or two. A budget is a measure of pace, never a reason to rush the player.

**Milestones.** Write each as an achievement and the level it gives, "or a victory of the same weight", and place it at a turning point or the climax, not at a place the party must visit. Keep milestones and money on separate lines.

**Revising.** When play makes a stage still ahead untrue (an antagonist dead, the goal changed, a stage put off), revise; never restart. Keep every stage, thread, secret, foreshadowing line and character arc that play did not contradict, word for word. Change only what is untrue. Grow anything new from what has happened and the threads already planted: a new antagonist or stage comes out of established events. Keep the arc's stakes, its theme and every beat not yet played, through a resolution: the arc still escalates and still ends. Re-anchor each milestone not yet awarded in the revised stages. A revision changes what the world does next, never what the player chose or what is canon.
```
